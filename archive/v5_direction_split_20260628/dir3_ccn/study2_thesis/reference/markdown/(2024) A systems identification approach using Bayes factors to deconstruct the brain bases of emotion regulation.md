Cognitive reappraisal is fundamental to cognitive therapies and everyday 
emotion regulation. Analyses using Bayes factors and an axiomatic 
systems identification approach identified four reappraisal-related 
components encompassing distributed neural activity patterns across 
two independent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies 
(n = 182 and n = 176): (1) an anterior prefrontal system selectively involved 
in cognitive reappraisal; (2) a fronto-parietal-insular system engaged by 
both reappraisal and emotion generation, demonstrating a general role 
in appraisal; (3) a largely subcortical system activated during negative 
emotion generation but unaffected by reappraisal, including amygdala, 
hypothalamus and periaqueductal gray; and (4) a posterior cortical system 
of negative emotion-related regions downregulated by reappraisal. These 
systems covaried with individual differences in reappraisal success and 
were differentially related to neurotransmitter binding maps, implicating 
cannabinoid and serotonin systems in reappraisal. These findings challenge 
‘limbic’-centric models of reappraisal and provide new systems-level targets 
for assessing and enhancing emotion regulation.
Emotion regulation is a fundamental process providing contextual and 
goal-directed influences on feelings, decision-making and behavior. 
It is crucial for everyday life and for mental and physical health1. Dys-
functional regulation is associated with a variety of mental health and 
substance use disorders2 as well as risk for chronic physical health con-
ditions. One of the most effective regulatory strategies is reappraisal, 
which involves reinterpreting the meaning of stimuli and events in 
service of a regulatory goal (for example, reducing negative emotion). 
Reinterpretations can include altering construals of actions, causal 
attributions of intention3, self-identification and distance and prospec-
tions about future consequences4, among other cognitive alterations5. 
Reappraisal is a core therapeutic mechanism in cognitive behavioral 
therapy and related treatments6.
Understanding the brain systems underlying emotion regula-
tion allows comparisons with systems involved in other forms of 
cognitive control7 and those affected by drugs8, brain damage, trauma 
and affective processes9, helping to understand how such factors 
affect regulatory control. Identifying reappraisal-related systems also 
provides new neurological targets for cognitive behavioral therapy, 
drug treatments, neurostimulation and other interventions. The 
major strategy for identifying reappraisal-related brain systems 
to date involves comparing responses to emotional stimuli with 
and without a regulatory goal (for example, ref. 7). Regions more 
active during regulation are typically thought of as generators of 
reappraisal-related processes, whereas those with decreased activity 
are labeled as regulation targets. Research using this approach has 
found that reappraisal activates several brain regions, including the 
lateral prefrontal cortex, superior temporal cortex and supplemen-
tary motor area (SMA), with decreases in ‘target’ regions principally 
in the amygdala10,11.

Results
Behavioral results
The three major reappraisal strategies participants used, from those 
defined by McRae et al.5, were ‘Change current circumstances’, ‘Real-
ity challenge’ and ‘Change future consequences’, indicating that 
strategies involving reinterpretation of the meaning of events were 
the major reappraisal strategy used (Fig. 1d). Negative affect ratings 
were substantially higher for negative than neutral pictures (‘Look 
negative’ − ‘Look neutral’; t = 63.8, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 3.37; Fig. 1e). 
Reappraisal substantially decreased negative affect compared to pas-
sive viewing (‘Regulate negative’ − ‘Look negative’; t = −17.5, P < 0.001, 
d = 0.92). Eighty-one percent of individual participants showed regu-
lation effects in the same direction as the group findings, indicating 
strong and robust effects.
Reaction times were also higher for ‘Look negative’ − ‘Look neutral’ 
(mean = 373 ms, t = 22.2, P < 0.001, BF > 10) and further increased for 
‘Regulate negative’ − ‘Look negative’ (mean = 108 ms, t = 6.42, P < 0.001, 
BF > 10), suggesting that both affective processing load and reappraisal 
increase the demand on information integration during evaluation 
(Supplementary Fig. 2).
Brain systems identified by BF analysis
How separate are neural mechanisms of reappraisal from those 
involved in emotion generation? We tested this question by identi-
fying ‘Reappraisal only’ brain regions activated during reappraisal 
(‘Regulate’ > ‘Look negative’, with 10:1 odds in favor of an effect, BF ≥ 10) 
but not emotion generation (‘Look negative’ = ‘Look neutral’, with 10:1 
odds in favor of the null, BF ≤ 0.1). We used the same BF thresholds in 
all subsequent tests and additionally required that voxels identified 
in each component map (for example, ‘Reappraisal only’) replicate all 
necessary effects in both studies independently (see ‘Consensus map’ 
subsection in Methods).

Several outstanding questions raised by these studies motivate 
the present work. The first concerns which emotion-related regions 
are affected by reappraisal. The amygdala is the region most consist-
ently downregulated by reappraisal of negative affect12, but studies and 
meta-analyses vary on whether amygdala activity is reduced by reap-
praisal13. In addition, many other key affective regions do not appear to 
be consistently influenced by reappraisal, including the periaqueductal 
gray (PAG)14,15, hypothalamus, ventral striatum and pallidum16. Other 
cortical areas involved in affect generation also do not appear to be 
consistently downregulated, and some are activated during reap-
praisal, including ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC17), anterior 
insula18, anterior midcingulate19 and orbitofrontal cortex20. The paucity 
of downregulation findings could be a power issue related to the small 
sample sizes typical of previous studies or could, alternatively, reflect a 
verum lack of influence, placing boundary conditions on the affective 
processes affected by reappraisal. In addition, affect generation is now 
thought to include many more regions (and distributed systems) than 
was appreciated in early emotion regulation studies21,22, and affect gen-
eration–related regions across the brain have not been systematically 
identified and tested for reappraisal effects. A broader approach that 
identifies areas engaged during negative emotion and then assesses 
which of these are downregulated and which are not is needed.
Another complication is that both emotion generation and reap-
praisal are thought to rely heavily on cognitive appraisal23, which is 
important for the construction of emotion24. Reappraisal may, thus, 
involve many of the same processes used to generate emotions but 
co-opted in service of regulatory goals. The ‘generators’ activated during 
reappraisal in previous studies may be specifically engaged during 
reappraisal, or they could be activated during negative emotion gene­
ration as well, signaling a role in common appraisal processes shared 
across emotion generation and regulation. Reappraisal-selective 
regions, should they exist, are likely associated with regulatory goals 
and may provide a key to understanding and assessing regulatory 
function in the brain. If reappraisal-selective regions can be identified, 
they could be measured to assess engagement in reappraisal, detect 
vulnerabilities and provide more specific brain targets for treatments.
Addressing these questions requires moving beyond traditional 
statistical hypothesis testing to evaluate evidence both for and against 
effects across multiple contrasts. For example, evaluating whether 
negative emotion-related regions are downregulated by reappraisal 
requires identifying emotion-relevant regions (for example, those 
activated by negative versus neutral images) and then testing evidence 
for and against reappraisal-related downregulation (for example, 
reductions with reappraisal versus passive viewing of negative images). 
Identifying reappraisal-specific regions involves establishing both 
a positive response to reappraisal and no difference (a null effect) 
when generating negative emotion without reappraisal. We refer to 
this as a systems identification approach, modeled on the axiomatic 
approach of Rutledge and Glimcher25. This approach takes the follow-
ing logical form: any reappraisal-specific region (1) must respond more 
strongly when reappraising negative images than when merely look-
ing at them and (2) must not respond differentially to negative versus 
neutral images in the absence of reappraisal demand. Such regions 
can be said to track a hypothetical cognitive process that responds 
selectively during engagement of regulatory goals. If brain regions 
with these properties can be identified, support is provided that such 
a process is a meaningful, identifiable neural component of emotion 
regulation. Evidence for and against each proposition can be assessed 
with Bayes factors (BFs)26. However, establishing evidence in favor of 
a null effect requires substantially larger samples than have typically 
been available27 (for example, n ≥ 120 to find a ratio of 10:1 in favor of 
the null; Supplementary Fig. 1).

We compared ‘Reappraisal only’ regions with those that fulfill the 
axioms for a ‘Common appraisal’ process, which are activated in the 
‘Look negative’ − ‘Look neutral’ contrast and further increase during 
reappraisal (‘Regulate negative’ − ‘Look negative’). ‘Common appraisal’ 
regions, shown in Fig. 2b, identified 14 regions, which is the largest set of 
brain regions of any component identified. These included dorsal and

Study 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

appraisal’ regions is shown in Fig. 2c. In lateral cortical areas, areas for

‘Common appraisal’ regions, suggesting a cortical gradient across areas 
sensitive specifically to goal-directed appraisal and those spontane-

or ‘Common appraisal’ (t = −0.76, P = 0.45, BF = 0.080) regions, with 
BFs less than 0.1 providing strong support for null findings (Supple-
mentary Fig. 4).

generation–related regions are downregulated during reappraisal.

We tested specifically for reappraisal effects in a priori amygdala 
subregions and PAG (Fig. 4). All amygdala subregions and the PAG 
responded robustly to negative images (‘Look negative’ − ‘Look neutral’, 
all P < 0.0001), indicating sensitivity to negative images, but no region 
was significantly downregulated by reappraisal (‘Regulate nega-
tive’ − ‘Look negative’, all BF < 0.1). Effects in the basolateral and super-
ficial amygdala were numerically in the opposite direction as predicted 
by the downregulation hypothesis. Separate models of early (0–2 s) and 
late (2–7 s) responses yielded similar findings (Supplementary Table 2). 
Additionally, we tested whether normative picture valence could influ-
ence amygdala regulation. Using a multi-level mixed-effect model, we 
found a null effect of ‘Regulation’ in all amygdala regions and a small 
‘Valence’ × ‘Regulation’ interaction in centromedial (CM) and superfi-
cial (SF) amygdala (d = 0.21 and 0.27, respectively, P < 0.05), indicating 
that the ‘Valence’ effect on amygdala activation is slightly attenuated 
(less negative) during reappraisal. The interaction in basolateral (BL) 
amygdala was not significant (d = 0.18 and P = 0.085). However, the BFs 
for these effects do not support a strong conclusion (BF < 1/3 in BL and 
close to 1 in CM and SF). Coupled with the null effects of ‘Regulation’, 
this suggests that amygdala activation could be marginally down-
regulated by reappraisal for the most aversive images but upregulated 
under reappraisal for the least aversive images—but if such effects exist, 
they are very small (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Beyond the ratings themselves, we found that differences in 
rating reaction times correlated with brain activity contrasts in the 
‘Common appraisal’ component for both emotion generation (‘Look

inconclusive evidence on the relationships between brain components 
and the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (Supplementary Fig. 9) 
and individual differences in negative emotion ratings (‘Look nega-

Functional annotation of reappraisal system components
To test the relationships between component maps and other mental 
functions, cognitive control, we assessed the association between our 
component maps and 50 psychological topic maps related to men-
tal processes—for example, face processing, inhibition and reward—
from https://neurosynth.org/ (ref. 30) (Supplementary Table 5). The 
‘Reappraisal only’ system was most strongly and selectively corre-
lated with the topics ‘response inhibition’, ‘executive function’ and 
‘empathy & interaction’, suggesting a close relationship between reap-
praisal and executive control of responses and task sets (Fig. 6). The 
‘Common appraisal’ system was most correlated with ‘working mem-
ory’, ‘task-switching’ and ‘cognitive conflict’ and unique to ‘cognitive 
conflict’, suggesting relationships with both executive function and 
value-related processes. ‘Non-modifiable emotion’ regions were most 
correlated with ‘emotional face’, ‘sleep & math’ and ‘working memory’ 
and unique to ‘sleep & math’. ‘Modifiable emotion’ regions were most 
strongly correlated with ‘object recognition’, ‘motion perception’ and 
‘spatial attention’ and unique to ‘object recognition’. The latter findings 
suggest an overlap between the brain regions subserving affect and 
cognition44,45 and in particular between modification of emotion and 
selection of object features and properties in images. As with all such 
maps, the findings are hypothesis generating and likely reflect biases 
in the literature on structure–function associations.

Discussion
Reappraisal is a foundational skill for regulating thoughts and behavior. 
However, to date, we have not had clear ways of separating brain activity 
related to reappraisal from activity related to emotion generation. As a 
result, it remains unclear whether reappraisal and affect generation are 
similar or distinct neural processes, and we have not had brain measures 
to assess when and how strongly reappraisal-related neural processes 
are engaged. In this study, we took a step toward addressing these ques-
tions. We identified four theoretical reappraisal-related processes 
and found support for the existence of brain systems supporting each: 
(1) a reappraisal-specific process not engaged during emotion genera-
tion; (2) a common appraisal process engaged by both emotion genera-
tion and reappraisal; (3) negative emotion-related areas unaffected by 
reappraisal; and (4) negative emotion-related areas downregulated 
by reappraisal. The use of larger samples (n > 350) made it feasible to 
identify brain regions related to these processes based on evidence for 
both activation differences and equivalencies (using BFs) with adequate 
power and generalizability across two independent cohorts.
We found that reappraisal engages distinct regions not activated 
during emotion generation alone. These were located anterior to 
‘Common appraisal’ regions in the prefrontal cortex, including adlPFC 
and avlPFC, and lateral and ventral to ‘Common appraisal’ regions in 
the parietal cortex, temporal cortex and cerebellum (including TPJ, 
aITG and cerebellar Crus I) and may play a unique role in developing 
human self-regulatory capacities. TPJ, aITG and adlPFC are among 
those with the largest expansion in humans versus other primates and 
during early-life human development46. TPJ, aITG and anterior PFC 
also appear to be among the most integrative, trans-modal areas in the 
brain47, positioned above unimodal sensory hierarchies and, thus, in a 
position to regulate them based on conceptual context.
Anterior dlPFC also has particularly high expression of human- 
accelerated genes48 and is related to meta-cognitive monitoring49, 
representation of contextual schemas50, future-oriented thought51 and 
goal maintenance and planning52. Studies of effective connectivity53  
and lesion studies54 also place it at the top level of cortical control

for multiple cognitive processes, including attention and memory 
retrieval, and high-level conceptual processes, including perceived 
agency and abstract relations56,57.
Although we identified reappraisal-selective regions, most regions 
activated during reappraisal were ‘Common appraisal’ regions also 
activated during negative emotion generation. These are presum-
ably involved in emotion-generating appraisals and may be involved 
in spontaneous self-regulation in the absence of explicit regulatory 
instructions58. These regions included medial prefrontal (dmPFC, 
aMCC, SMA and precuneus), lateral fronto-parietal (dlPFC, vlPFC, SPL, 
IPL and STS) and anterior insular cortices. These regions have been 
identified in previous meta-analyses of both emotion generation59,60 
and reappraisal11,61 and are co-activated in studies of emotion62. These 
regions are located higher in cortical hierarchies63 than sensorimotor 
regions but below reappraisal-selective regions.
Among ‘Common appraisal’ regions, dlPFC and vlPFC are most 
strongly associated with self-regulation10, and vlPFC correlates with 
upregulation of NAc and reappraisal success37 (a finding that we rep-
licate here; see discussion below). Although sometimes considered 
‘cold’ regions involved in context representation and information 
selection64, they may play a direct role in affective appraisal and regula-
tion. For example, dlPFC directly correlates with trial-by-trial ratings of 
food craving during a self-regulation task65, and vlPFC correlates with 
trial-by-trial ratings of negative affect and social rejection66. Other 
‘Common appraisal’ regions are also thought to integrate somatic, 
emotional and cognitive information to enable subjective affective 
awareness, particularly the anterior insula18, dmPFC67 and aMCC68.
In the context of generalizability, ‘Common appraisal’ and 
‘Reappraisal only’ regions were correlated with individual differences in 
reappraisal success (‘Reappraisal only’ particularly so) and generalized 
across strategies (Supplementary Fig. 11). Several ‘Common appraisal’ 
regions—including preSMA, vlPFC, dlPFC and insula—are also engaged 
by other emotion regulation strategies, such as expressive suppression 
and distraction11,13. ‘Reappraisal only’ and ‘Common appraisal’ regions 
were associated with Neurosynth meta-analytic topic maps for working 
memory and response inhibition, providing a link between appraisal 
and other forms of cognitive control. Associations with neurotrans-
mitter maps31 show that prefrontal and parietal ‘Reappraisal only’ and 
‘Common appraisal’ regions are among the cortical regions with the 
highest levels of serotonin receptors (including 5HT1b and 5HT2a) and 
cannabinoid (CB1) receptors. Serotonin systems are broadly implicated 
in mood, aggression, sleep and pain and have been associated with 
psychedelic drug effects (especially 5-HT2A)69 and psychotherapy 
(5-HT1B70 and 5-HT1A71). CB1 is also implicated in mood regulation72. 
These studies point to common substrates of appraisal-based and 
pharmacological interventions in brain neurochemistry.
These reappraisal-related regions provide new brain targets for 
interventions, including psychotherapy, self-regulation training, 
neuro­stimulation and neurofeedback73,74. Using areas activated during 
reappraisal as targets may not be an effective strategy, because most 
areas activated by reappraisal are also activated by negative emotion 
generation and could increase negative affect as well as potentiate 
reappraisal. Instead, focusing on ‘Reappraisal only’ regions may be a 
better approach.
Another goal of emotion regulation research is to understand 
which affect-generating processes are targeted and downregulated 
by reappraisal and related cognitive interventions. In previous work, 
amygdala was reported as the primary target of emotion regulation10,75 
and is frequently taken as an indicator of regulatory efficacy76. However, 
the amygdala and other subcortical affect-related regions—particularly 
PAG, hypothalamus and ventral striatum and pallidum—were identified 
as ‘Non-modifiable emotion’ regions in the present study and were nei-
ther influenced by reappraisal nor correlated with reappraisal success. 
This might indicate that subcortical affective processes are less modifi-
able by reappraisal than previously thought. They may be more rapidly 
engaged, occurring before cognitive intervention is applied77, and be 
more automatic and less susceptible to conceptual information78.
Previous studies are actually surprisingly mixed on whether they 
find amygdala downregulation. Although early studies and meta- 
analyses identified amygdala decreases10,75, many recent studies79,80 
and meta-analyses11,81 do not (see also ref. 82), and some report 
reappraisal-related amygdala increases79,83. Importantly, neither indi-
vidual studies nor coordinate-based imaging meta-analyses (CBMAs) 
formally consider (1) the base rate of null findings across studies and 
(2) analytic flexibility that can increase false-positive rates. For example, 
some studies finding amygdala downregulation do not specify 
amygdala regions of interest a priori, as we did in the present study, 
but rather rely on tests across hundreds of amygdala voxels at uncor-
rected thresholds. Unlike traditional meta-analyses, CBMAs do not 
formally test the average amygdala effect size across all studies, and 
meta-analytic findings are only indirectly influenced by the accumula-
tion of null results. In addition, the use of lower thresholds in amygdala 
inflates the number of positive findings in amygdala relative to other 
regions and reduces the effects of null findings on the meta-analytic 
results. Thus, there is a need for highly powered confirmatory tests 
of precisely defined regions and analytic procedures84, such as those 
provided here. With n = 358 participants, our study is highly powered to 
detect amygdala reductions at the voxel level, with 80% power to detect 
even small effects of d = 0.2 or greater at typical q < 0.05 FDR-corrected 
voxelwise thresholds (P < 0.002, two tailed85).
In addition, amygdala downregulation may depend on the strategy 
used, goals to increase or decrease emotion and how effectively strate-
gies are deployed. For example, Dorfel et al.13 found that detachment 
was superior to reappraisal in decreasing amygdala activity, and two 
meta-analyses suggest that psychological distancing produces more 
consistent amygdala decreases than reinterpretation81 or extinc-
tion86. The dominant strategies that our participants adopted were 
reinterpretation-based, including altered appraisals of images’ reality 
(‘reality challenge’), current circumstances and future consequences 
and the agency of those depicted (Fig. 1d). We did not find relationships 
between amygdala decreases and either the strategy used or reap-
praisal success, although such relationships were significant for other 
regions, including decreases in the ‘Modifiable emotion’ cortical sys-
tem and increases in ‘Reappraisal only’, ‘Common appraisal’ and other 
regions, including NAc (Fig. 5). However, we did not systematically 
investigate less common strategies, such as psychological distancing.
Other factors may influence the strength of amygdala downregu-
lation, including the timing of regulation effects, previous exposure 
or practice reappraising particular stimuli and potential interac-
tions with population characteristics, such as age and sex. In terms 
of timing, Pierce et al.80 found reappraisal effects only during a late 
post-image-viewing period (see also ref. 87). In the present study, we 
did not find regulation effects on either early or late picture-viewing 
periods, but the study was not designed to investigate effects during 
the post-viewing rating period. Other studies have found that amyg-
dala responses during passive viewing are reduced when participants 
previously reappraised a particular image88,89. Thus, practice regulat-
ing specific stimuli may be important. Studies vary on whether they 
report how items are re-used, and future studies should report this 
explicitly. In terms of effects of stimulus valence, we did not find that 
amygdala downregulation depended appreciably on how aversive an 
image was, similar to previous results90, although we did find weak 
evidence for stronger effects on the most aversive images. Our images 
were restricted to a narrow range of highly aversive images, and this 
effect should be studied across a wider range of stimulus valence in 
future studies.

and populations for other reasons as well. Our population in the

The regions most strongly downregulated by reappraisal 
(‘Modifiable emotion regions’) were in the visual and somatosensory 
cortices. One explanation may be that participants shifted their atten-
tion from emotional to non-emotional content during reappraisal91, 
leading to decreased activation in visual cortices during reappraisal. 
However, previous research has demonstrated successful reappraisal 
even when visual attention is controlled92,93. In addition, shifting atten-
tion from emotional to non-emotional content results in decreased 
activity in early visual cortex in previous studies94, whereas ‘Modifiable 
emotion’ regions are localized to higher-order visual regions (for exam-
ple, V3, V4, V7, MT, VO, MST and MT) and parietal cortex. These regions 
are related to visual properties, such as object and scene representa-
tion95, but also encode semantic meaning and context96. Therefore, 
reappraisal may impact semantic and conceptual representations in 
these areas rather than simply reflecting shifts in attended location.
Extending this view, visual and somatosensory streams are increas-
ingly seen as integral to affect generation. Recent work suggests that 
human sensation and emotion are inextricably linked97, and predicting 
perceptual and action demands is an essential part of embodied views 
of emotion98. Previous studies found that emotion triggers distinct 
neural representations in the visual cortex99,100. These neural repre-
sentations are valence specific101,102, and different visual cortical pat-
terns encode at least five distinguishable emotion categories99. Other 
types of affect appear to be embedded in early sensory pathways as 
well. For example, a recent study found a superior collicular-visual 
pathway specific to encoding visually generated negative affect, an 
inferior collicular-auditory pathway specific to aurally generated nega-
tive affect and separate somatosensory representations for thermal 
and mechanical pain22. Additionally, somatosensory and posterior 
insular areas downregulated by reappraisal may be related to intero-
ception103. A recent study on emotion regulation also found a similar 
downregulation104.
Other ‘Non-modifiable emotion’ regions are distributed through-
out the brain, including dlPFC, OFC, IPS, premotor cortex and early 
visual areas, implying that cognitive reappraisal affects only a subset of 
affect generation processes. For example, some parts of dlPFC (particu-
larly the inferior frontal junction and the inferior frontal sulcus), dmPFC 
and OFC (Brodmann’s area 11) are engaged during negative affect but 
not modifiable. Some dlPFC areas that overlap ‘Common appraisal’ are 
similar to those that predict trial-to-trial variation in affective value105. 
OFC regions are also among those most closely associated with appeti-
tive and aversive value20, particularly in relation to contextual variables, 
and in value-based reinforcement learning106. The relative ‘immunity’ 
of these regions to reappraisal suggests that cognitive reappraisal may 
not be entirely effective in regulating the evaluation of affective stimuli. 
In addition, these findings update the commonly held view that pre-
frontal cortices are ‘cold’ cognitive structures particularly influenced 
by self-regulation and provide evidence for a more nuanced pattern.
In conclusion, our findings indicate that emotion generation and 
reappraisal depend on many of the same cortical regions, in line with 
psychological theories that posit overlap between affective appraisal 
and reappraisal. However, reappraisal involves a distinct set of brain 
regions situated at the highest levels of cortical hierarchies and is 
associated with executive cognitive functions, particularly managing 
hierarchies of goals over long timescales. They are also enriched in sero-
tonin (5HT-1B and 5HT-2A) and CB1 receptors, which may preferentially 
subserve and interact with reappraisal. The target systems downregu-
lated by reappraisal are limited mainly to perceptual, semantic and 
interoceptive processes related to the construction of affective value. 
We found no effects on subcortical drivers of affective experience (for 
example, amygdala, PAG and hypothalamus) or on lateral prefrontal 
and orbitofrontal areas related to value construction. These findings 
inform on the systems involved in reappraisal and those involved in 
altered affective experience and provide new targets for assessment 
and intervention.

Methods
Participants and paradigm
Participants were derived from two study cohorts: the Adult Health 
and Behavior project, phase 2 (AHAB-2; Study 1), and the Pittsburgh 
Imaging Project (PIP; Study 2)43. Both studies recruited participants 
from the Greater Pittsburgh area. In total, 182 participants (95 males 
and 87 females, mean age = 43.45 ± 7.26 years) from Study 1 and 176 
participants (87 males and 89 females, mean age = 40.58 ± 6.33 years) 
from Study 2 are included in the current study. These participants 
completed an emotional picture viewing and reappraisal task. Details 
of the task were provided in a previous study112. During the task, par-
ticipants viewed 30 unpleasant and 15 neutral International Affective 
Picture System (IAPS) images113. First, a 2-s cue (‘Look’ or ‘Decrease’) 
instructed participants to either look and attend to images (‘Look’) or 
decrease their negative feelings about aversive images by reapprais-
ing their circumstances and content (‘Decrease’). Following the cue, 
images were presented for 7 s. After picture presentation, participants 
had 4 s to rate their negative emotion on a five-point scale (1 = neutral, 
5 = extremely unpleasant), followed by a randomized rest period (1–3 s). 
E-Prime software was used to present the task and collect behavioral 
ratings. The task comprised 15 ‘Look neutral’ trials, 15 ‘Look negative’ 
trials and 15 ‘Decrease negative’ trials. Eleven unpleasant images for 
‘Decrease negative’ trials, 13 unpleasant images for ‘Look negative’ trials 
and two neutral images overlapped across Study 1 and Study 2. Images 
were shown in a pseudo-random order. No more than two identical 
instructional cues (‘Look’ or ‘Decrease’) were presented consecutively. 
No more than four negative images were shown consecutively. Data 
collection and analysis were not performed blinded to the conditions 
of the experiments. In Study 1, mean normative arousal and valence 
values for pictures assigned to ‘Look negative’, ‘Regulate negative’ and 
‘Look neutral’ conditions were (6.16 ± 0.52, 2.13 ± 0.37), (6.21 ± 0.67, 
2.01 ± 0.35) and (2.65 ± 0.40, 5.12 ± 0.57), respectively. Values in Study 2 
were (6.14 ± 0.53, 2.10 ± 0.37), (6.37 ± 0.52, 2.02 ± 0.36) and (3.44 ± 0.37, 
5.97 ± 0.35), respectively. Aversive images were thus similar in norma-
tive valence, and substantially more aversive than neutral images.
One concern regarding this task paradigm is that participants 
might not attend to the pictures and look away during picture viewing 
period. Because we did not record eye tracking or related data to con-
firm fixation on the pictures for the entire viewing period, we applied 
two additional analyses to test the potential influence of eye movement. 
In the first analysis, we compared our main results with a Neurosynth 
term map that associates with ‘eye movement’ and found limited spatial 
correlation (Supplementary Fig. 12a). In the second analysis, we applied 
a recently developed deep neural network model for predicting eye 
movements based on fMRI data, DeepMREye114. We analyzed correla-
tions between model-derived eye movement estimates and task 
contrasts of interest. Specifically, we first calculated a measure of 
absolute eye movement (change in gaze position) given DeepMREye 
position coordinates x and y: m =√(xt−xt−1)
2+(yt−yt−1)
2 , where t indexes 
time. Second, we regressed m on the task regressors for each partici-
pant and calculated contrasts of interest. Third, we performed 
a second-level random effects analysis on the contrasts, assessing 
‘Regulate negative’ versus ‘Look negative’ and ‘Look negative’ versus 
‘Look neutral’ differences in absolute eye movement. We did not find 
strong evidence that eye movement is systematically different across 
task conditions (Supplementary Fig. 12b). These results suggest that 
eye movement does not play a substantial role in our findings, although 
the absence of eye movement data to validate and fine-tune the model 
for the second analysis is a limitation.
Reappraisal strategies
A post-task inventory was administered to participants to assess dif-
ferent emotion regulation strategies that may have been used during 
the fMRI task paradigm, as detailed previously115. As part of the inven-
tory, participants were asked to describe the strategies that they used 
to change their feelings in the ‘Regulate negative’ condition. Using an 
open-ended response format, participants were asked: ‘What sorts of 
things did you tell yourself to try to help you feel differently in response 
to the negative pictures?’ Open-ended responses were coded accord-
ing to standardized procedures detailed in previous work5. Responses 
were coded as reappraisal if they fell into any of these categories: (1) 
‘explicitly positive’, (2) ‘change in current circumstances’, (3) ‘reality 
challenge’, (4) ‘change of future consequences’, (5) ‘ascribing agency 
to a person capable of changing the circumstances depicted in the 
scene’, (6) ‘distancing’, (7) ‘problem-solving’, (8) ‘acceptance’ and (9) 
‘non-specific reappraisal’. A final category was ‘failed to reappraise’, 
which included a failure to use any form of reappraisal (for example, 
looking away or not thinking about the IAPS scene, using suppression 
or using a strategy that was not cognitive).
fMRI data acquisition and pre-processing
MRI imaging data from Study 1 and Study 2 were collected on the 
same 3T Trio TIM whole-body scanner (Siemens) using a 12-channel, 
phased-array head coil. fMRI acquisition parameters were as follows: 
FOV = 205 × 205 mm, matrix size = 64 × 64, TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 28 ms 
and FA = 90°.
fMRI data were pre-processed with statistical parametric map-
ping software (SPM12; http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). Func-
tional images were realigned to the first image of the series with a 
six-parameter rigid body transformation. Before realignment, slice 
timing correction was applied to the IAPS fMRI task data to account 
for acquisition time variation in this event-related design. Realigned 
images were co-registered to each participant’s skull-stripped and 
biased-corrected MPRAGE T1 image. Co-registered images were 
then normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space and 
interpolated to 2 × 2 × 2-mm voxels. Normalized images were then 
smoothed by a 6-mm full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) Gauss-
ian kernel.
First-level analysis
Within-subject task effects and contrasts were estimated with a gen-
eral linear model (GLM). The task conditions (‘Look neutral’, ‘Look 
negative’ and ‘Regulate negative’) were separately modeled with 
boxcar functions and convolved with the canonical hemodynamic 
response function (HRF) using SPM12 software. Regressors included 
one for the picture-viewing period in each task condition and one 
for each of the cue and rating periods collapsed across task condi-
tions. We chose this method to minimize multicolinearity between 
the picture-viewing regressors and to maximize stability of individual 
person-level estimates. To test the robustness of the method’s find-
ings, we also repeated the analyses with a second first-level model that 
included separate regressors for the ‘Look’ and ‘Regulate’ cues. The 
main results remained robust and very similar to those that we report 
here, with no major changes in the conclusions (see Supplementary 
Fig. 4 for cue period results). The timing and duration of stimulus 
epochs were modeled according to the onset and duration of the stimu-
lus presention for each event. A high-pass filter of 1/180 Hz was applied 
to remove low-frequency temporal drift. In addition, nuisance regres-
sors included six head motion parameters (x, y, z, roll, pitch and yaw), 
spike indicator regressors for potential outliers (with a regressor for 
each individual potential outlier coded as 1 and other volumes coded 
as 0), and time series of white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. Time 
series of fMRI responses within canonical eroded white matter and 
cerebrospinal fluid masks were extracted using CANlab tools (https://
github.com/canlab/CanlabCore). The six head motion covariates were 
extracted from realignment parameters. Images were defined as spikes 
if they were outside the 95% confidence regions of the cloud of images 
in multidimensional space (Mahalanobis distance). The number of 
excluded potential outliers per participant was 19 ± 7.7 for Study 2 and 
17 ± 8.0 for Study 1, representing approximately 5% of the fMRI images.

The system identification approach

proposed system components. Each process in our theoretical

addition, these voxels have positive activation in the ‘Look negative’ 
condition.

‘Emotion generation’ and BF > 10 for ‘Reappraisal’ with a negative effect 
(t < 0, reduced activity during reappraisal). In addition, these voxels 
have positive activation in the ‘Look negative’ condition.
Note that the thresholds are asymmetric to the null and alterna-
tive hypothesis. The null is bounded at a minimum where the means 
are identical (t = 0). The range for t value would be narrower (BF < 0.1) 
for the null compared to strong evidence in favor of the alternative 
(BF > 10). This range is dependent on sample size. A sample size of 
n = 120 is the minimum number to find a stable t range for the null 
hypothesis (Supplementary Fig. 1). A larger sample size is better to 
find robust evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.
Consensus maps
As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, identified systems from Study 1 
and Study 2 were located in similar regions but did not completely 
overlap. To identify voxels in similar locations across both studies, we 
first conducted spatial smoothing using a 3-mm Gaussian kernel on 
each system component map (binary map). Next, to test the extent of 
overlap between Study 1 and Study 2, we multiplied the voxel values 
in smoothed maps for Study 1 and Study 2 to yield a product map. 
Higher product map values indicate higher degree of spatial overlap 
on a certain voxel. A binary consensus map was constructed by setting 
a minimum threshold for the product map. In the present study, we 
used 0.01 as a threshold to keep as much spatial similarity and a similar 
number of identified voxels in the consensus map compared with the 
individual study maps. In both individual studies and the consensus 
map, we retained clusters ≥15 contiguous voxels.
To characterize the consensus maps, we tested if the voxels 
revealed in consensus maps overlap with or are close to the identified 
system from Study 1 and Study 2. Specifically, we tested the distance 
between voxels in the consensus map and their nearest voxels in each 
of the thresholded BF maps from Study 1 and Study 2. For each voxel, 
the maximum distance across Study 1 and Study 2 was recorded. We 
computed this maximum distance for all voxels in the consensus map. 
This distance was less than 4.5 mm for all consensus component maps, 
indicating a voxel in both studies within 4.5 mm of each consensus map 
voxel. Specifically, the maximum deviating distance for each system 
component was ‘Common appraisal’, 4.4 mm; ‘Reappraisal only’, 4 mm; 
‘Non-modifiable emotion’, 4.4 mm; and ‘Modifiable emotion’, 4.4 mm.
Neurosynth dataset
To provide an unbiased and data-driven based method to annotate 
the identified system components, we used the Neurosynth database 
(http://neurosynth.org), which relies on reported activation pattern 
from 11,406 studies30. The large scale of the Neurosynth dataset is 
able to compensate for the possible random error reported in a single 
study, providing robust assignment of brain function in a given region.
We selected 50 psychological topics (for example, face process-
ing, inhibition and reward) from a set of 100 topics (V4-topics-100) 
based on 11,406 studies, excluding topics related to psychological 
content such as ‘stimulation’, ‘task’ or ‘paradigm’. The topic maps 
are computed using reverse inference, which denotes probability 
of the topic given activation. Detailed methods to generate these 
topic maps can be found in Poldrack et al.117. To calculate the spatial 
similarity between topic maps and system components, topic maps 
were resampled into the same space as system components. Spa-
tial similarity was assessed via Pearson correlation between topic 
maps and identified systems in a pairwise fashion. Due to the large 
variation in voxel counts across system components (‘Reappraisal 
only’, ‘Common appraisal’, ‘Non-modifiable emotion generation’ and 
‘Modifiable emotion generation’), the correlation coefficients varied 
across components. To eliminate systematic spatial extent-related 
differences, we normalized the correlation coefficients within each 
system using Z-score normalization to make sure that each system 
component obtains the same average correlation with 50 topics, 
which represents the relative correlation across all topics. A principal 
component analysis was conducted on the normalized correlation 
matrix, and the relative locations of topics and system components 
were plotted in the space of the first two principal components. 
The top three correlated topics for each system component are 
displayed.
We also reported the topics most selectively associated with one 
system component compared to the others’ components. Specifi-
cally, a selectivity score for each topic was computed as the difference 
between the best correlated system component and the second best 
one. For each system component, we identified the topic that had the 
highest selectivity.

PET tracer neurotransmitter receptor density dataset
We investigated the spatial correlation between identified system com-
ponents and a series of neurotransmitter receptor/transporter maps 
from a PET tracer dataset31. This dataset includes mean binding maps 
from 36 PET studies, including 19 receptors and transporters across nine 
different neurotransmitter systems from a total of approximately 1,200 
healthy individuals (Supplementary Table 6). Multiple maps from inde-
pendent studies were available for a subset of receptors/transporters. 
Neurotransmitter receptor/transporter maps were resampled to the 
same space as the system component maps for the purpose of assess-
ing spatial similarity. A Pearson’s correlation across voxels was calcu-
lated as a measure of spatial similarity between system component 
maps and each of the 36 neurotransmitter receptor/transporter maps. 
To ensure the reliability of spatial correlation, we performed boot-
strap analysis within both Study 1 and Study 2, resampling individual 
participants’ contrast maps within each study and re-generating the 
four consensus system components (across both studies) based on 
100 bootstrap samples for each study. Spatial correlations were com-
puted between the bootstrapped consensus map and each PET tracer 
neurotransmitter map. The mean spatial correlations and their standard 
deviations across bootstrap samples (an estimate of the standard 
error of the correlation) are reported in radar plots and results.
This analysis does not assess error variability in the neurochemical 
maps themselves; therefore, we adopted a strategy of assessing 
replications of neurotransmitter–emotion regulation map associa-
tions across individual PET studies, which were available for many 
neurotransmitters (CB1, 5HT1a/1b/2a, 5HTT, VAChT, NET, mu-opioids, 
mGluR5, GABA, D1, D2 and DAT), and we interpreted only associations 
that were replicated across PET studies. Agreement across PET studies 
appeared to be high in most cases, although there are discrepancies 
(the same system can be assessed with different tracers, with some-
times divergent results).
