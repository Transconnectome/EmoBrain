Abstract
The ability to quickly and accurately perceive external emotional 
stimuli — events in the environment that evoke changes in feelings, 
physiology and behaviour — is vital for adaptive social interactions 
and effective decision making in everyday life. Contemporary theories 
of emotional perception emphasize the influence of top-down 
information (such as prior knowledge and context) in shaping the 
perception of emotional stimuli. However, experimental research 
has mainly focused on the automatic, bottom-up aspects that are 
driven by the stimuli themselves (such as salience). Research in the 
adjacent field of visual perception has used behavioural, computational 
and neuroimaging techniques to reveal how prior knowledge aids 
perception in a top-down manner. In this Review, we explore studies that 
leverage similar methods to demonstrate how top-down influences —  
including social and emotional attention, expectations and context —  
shape the perception of emotional stimuli. In doing so, we aim to 
promote the development of comprehensive models that incorporate 
top-down factors with bottom-up factors to explain the perception of 
emotional stimuli.

Introduction
Picture yourself strolling down a trail in the desert when you spot a 
thin elongated shape on the trail ahead. Your brain receives a complex 
spatiotemporal pattern of light from the retina, a pattern that could 
have originated from a myriad of sources: ropes, rocks, twigs, snakes 
or shadows. To resolve this ambiguity, your brain uses environmental 
cues and prior knowledge of the context to navigate the uncertainty 
and arrive at a definitive interpretation of the ambiguous sensory data: 
the perception of a snake (Fig. 1).
Because an emotional stimulus such as a snake is a sensory input 
that can trigger an emotional response, it is hypothesized that human 
perceptual systems prioritize its processing because of its evolutionary 
importance1,2. This evolutionary perspective is rooted in the idea that 
emotions have evolved as adaptive mechanisms over millions of years 
to help organisms respond to various challenges and opportunities 
in their environments. From this perspective, it might be argued that 
bottom-up sensory evidence emanating from the snake’s features, such 
as elongated shape, glistening body and gliding motion, is enough to 
trigger its fast recognition. This fast recognition is similar to automatic 
attention capture by stimuli with salient physical features such as bright 
colours, high contrast or fast movement3.
However, perception — the interpretation of sensory data ema-
nating from the external world — does not depend only on bottom-up 
sensory information; it is an active conjuring of a world that is shaped 
by bottom-up and top-down influences. The view that internally driven 
top-down influences guide perception has been espoused by ancient 
Eastern texts describing how perception is distorted by ignorance, 
desires and past experiences4, Western philosophers describing per-
ception as the brain’s unconscious inference of sensory causes based 
on past experience 5, experimental psychologists manipulating con-
text, attention and expectation to shape perception6, neuroscientists 
examining descending feedback neural pathways7,8, cyberneticians 
describing the hierarchical control of perception9 and computational 
scientists modelling perception as an inferential process10,11. Contem-
porary models of basic visual perception prominently feature the

In this Review, we apply insights from basic visual perception and 
cognitive neuroscience research on the perception of non-emotional 
or neutral stimuli to further understanding of the perception of emo-
tional stimuli. We argue that the top-down mechanisms highlighted in 
the literature overlap with the mechanisms of emotion perception. We 
begin by reviewing how the conceptualization of emotion can influ-
ence views of how emotional stimuli are perceived. Next, we discuss 
how top-down emotion-related factors might be incorporated into 
quantitative models of decision making, including signal-detection 
theory, drift-diffusion modelling, and predictive coding or active 
inference frameworks. Subsequently, we describe the behavioural 
paradigms that are employed to illustrate top-down influences on 
perception of emotional stimuli, highlighting differences from pre-
vious research. We then review empirical research that reveals the 
psychological, computational and neural mechanisms by which 
top-down emotional and social factors facilitate the perception of 
emotional stimuli. We hope to spur the development of comprehen-
sive and ecologically valid models in which perception of emotional 
stimuli is shaped by an interaction of emotion-related top-down and 
bottom-up factors.
Frameworks for top-down influences  
on emotion perception
An emotional stimulus is defined by its ability to evoke an emotional 
response, and the perception of such a stimulus, along with the emo-
tion it elicits, mutually influence each other30. Hence, existing theories 
regarding the elicitation of emotion are relevant to understanding 
perception of emotional stimuli (Box 1). However, the research covered 
in this Review focuses specifically on the perceptual impacts. First, we 
clarify the experimental paradigms usually used to study top-down 
influences on emotion perception and their limitations. Following this 
discussion, we draw on basic visual perception research to describe 
distinct behavioural paradigms suitable for examining both explicit and 
implicit top-down influences on emotion perception. Finally, we review 
the computational models that can quantify these top-down effects.
Box 1 | Theoretical frameworks of emotion
 
Emotions, such as fear or joy, are constructs that cannot be directly 
observed but are instead inferred from various observable indicators 
such as language expression (for instance, a self-report of fear), 
overt behaviour (such as freezing), and physiological changes (such 
as increased heartrate), as described in the three-systems model of 
emotion195–200. Importantly, although an emotion can be inferred from 
these observable features, it should not be equated or reduced to 
them, an error that frequently occurs in the literature201.
Early theoretical perspectives on emotion were grounded 
in the notion that emotions evolved to adaptively facilitate the 
responses of organisms to diverse challenges and opportunities 
in their environments1,2. This evolutionary perspective emphasizes 
universality202,203, phylogenetic continuity204 and automaticity118 of 
the emotional response. It follows from accounts that the perception 
of emotional stimuli is prioritized because of their evolutionary 
salience205 and that it operates at an automatic level98,206. This 
emotional stimulus-driven prioritization aligns with bottom-up 
influences in basic visual perception, which are driven by a host of 
factors, including the physical features of the stimulus, grouping 
due to gestalt principles, and dissimilarity from the surrounding 
background or ‘pop-out’12,207. Behavioural paradigms tend to 
capitalize on the automatic processing of emotional stimuli by 
focusing on their influence on incidental cognitive processes208,209. 
This influence is assumed to stem from the rapid, involuntary and 
unconscious processing of emotional stimuli via specialized neural 
circuits210–215. The evolutionary approach has made considerable 
inroads into clinical and social psychology, evident in the adoption 
of paradigms that emphasize the role of bottom-up factors in 
understanding individual differences, psychopathology209,216 and 
social cognition217.
In contrast to the evolutionary accounts of emotion1, later theories 
emphasize cognitive and contextual involvement in the generation 
or interpretation of the eliciting stimulus and/or related physiological 
changes. Among these theories, the two-factor theory of emotion 
proposes that a stimulus triggers a physiological response that is 
interpreted and labelled according to context, yielding an emotion218. 
Cognitive mediational theory posits that a stimulus is followed by 
cognitive appraisal, ultimately resulting in physiological changes 
and the emotion219. Finally, the component process model proposes 
that emotions are an emergent property of dynamic interactions 
between different components that include physiological changes, 
cognitive appraisal, overt behaviour, subjective feelings and action 
tendencies220.
According to a more contemporary theory of constructed 
emotion, the brain maintains an internal generative model of 
emotions based on past knowledge and makes predictions regarding 
sensory input that it expects to receive from the body’s internal 
(interoceptive) and external (exteroceptive) environment221. The 
brain compares the expected input with the actual input it receives: a 
match results in an emotion, whereas a mismatch yields a prediction 
error that updates the predictions, and a new emotional experience 
is then constructed. In the context of this theory, there are no unique 
hardwired emotions but a continuous spectrum of emotional 
experiences defined along the dimensions of valence (ranging from 
pleasant to unpleasant) and arousal (ranging from low to high)222. 
Any categorization imposed on the affective experience is due to 
the brain’s use of past experiences, cultural norms and language to 
create categories for emotions such as anger, fear and happiness. 
A related framework, the predictive processing framework, argues 
that emotions arise from inferential processes that combine 
sensory (interoceptive, proprioceptive and exteroceptive) signals 
with top-down predictions and their main function is to reduce 
precision-weighted prediction errors, where positive valence reflects 
better-than-predicted slopes of error minimization223.
Overall, theories of emotion shape the views of researchers on 
how emotional stimuli are perceived. Evolutionary accounts that 
emphasize automatic hardwired stimulus-driven patterns leave little 
room for the influence of top-down factors, whereas appraisal and 
constructivist theories that emphasize the role of language, learning, 
cognitive processes and context include top-down factors.

Experimental paradigms
At the core of the emotion theories that guide emotion perception 
research lies the dual-process framework, which permeates various 
branches of contemporary psychology31–33. This framework describes 
emotions as arising from the interplay between automatic (implicit, 
unconscious) processes triggered by external stimuli and controlled 
(explicit, voluntary and goal-driven) processes that modulate these 
automatic responses. Emotion theories differ in emphasis, with 
older ones favouring automatic processing of emotional stimuli in 
a bottom-up manner and more contemporary theories prioritizing 
the role of more controlled processes in emotion perception (Box 1).
The differential emphasis in these emotion theories is reflected 
in the different task paradigms used to examine top-down influences 
on emotion perception. Despite the more contemporary theoretical 
focus on top-down factors such as context, predictions, language and 
learning, there remains a persistent inclination to rely on behavioural 
paradigms that prioritize the bottom-up, involuntary aspects of emo-
tion perception. For instance, several studies use paradigms in which 
the independent variable involves manipulating cognitive strategies — 
explicit or implicit — to inhibit emotional stimulus processing and the 
dependent variable reflects the extent to which automatic processing 
of the stimulus persists despite these top-down efforts34,35. In other 
paradigms, the independent variable is the implicit or involuntary 
perception of an emotional stimulus and the dependent variable is the 
facilitated detection of a simultaneously or subsequently presented 
neutral stimulus (for reviews see refs. 36,37). Such studies have inves-
tigated how the emotional aspect of a stimulus (such as the expression 
of the face) facilitates perception of a simultaneously presented neutral 
aspect (such as the gender of the face38), a subsequently presented neu-
tral target (such as the contrast of a Gabor patch39,40) or a subsequently 
presented peripheral probe (as in the dot probe task41–44). In these 
studies, the independent variable is neither inherently emotional nor 
structured to guide perception of the emotional stimulus.
Related studies have examined the top-down influence of experi-
mentally induced or naturally varying emotional mood on the percep-
tion of emotional and neutral stimuli37,45. For instance, these studies 
have examined the influence of an emotional state on the perception 
of a mood-related46 or mood-unrelated neutral stimulus47–49. However, 
there are several methodological challenges that complicate efforts 
to isolate top-down influences on perception in these studies50. One 
challenge is that the source of top-down influences in these studies is 
unclear — an emotional response elicited by an emotional stimulus or 
an emotional state induced through mood manipulation encompasses 
a diverse array of changes in subjective feelings, physiological arousal, 
cognitive processes and behavioural responses. Moreover, very few 
tasks can be unequivocally labelled as purely measures of perception50. 
Hence, it has been proposed that the impact of mood in these studies 
might be on memory, on attention or on downstream variables such 
as decision confidence and response bias rather than on perception50. 
For these reasons, we do not review these studies here.
Instead, we focus on studies in which the independent variable is 
explicit or implicit information regarding an emotional stimulus and 
the dependent variable is the perception of the stimulus. Examples 
include studies that examine whether explicit pre-stimulus information 
regarding emotional stimuli, such as their relevance51–53, probability54,55 
and location56, facilitate perception (Fig. 2). These studies are inspired 
by basic perception research that differentiates between top-down 
attention directed towards stimuli relevant to one’s goals and expec-
tations regarding how probable a stimulus is. The former is examined 
via top-down spatial, feature and object-related cues that guide atten-
tion to locations or features of target stimuli57–59. The latter involves 
expectation-related cues that indicate the probability of upcoming 
stimuli13,60.
Although attention and expectation cues explicitly guide percep-
tion, the context in which the target appears implicitly influences how 
it is perceived. Therefore, we also review studies in which contextual 
information can implicitly facilitate perception of an emotional aspect 
of the context (for instance, bodily context aids the perception of 
expected emotional expressions61) and explicit search of embedded 
emotional targets62–64. This implicit influence is similar to how objects, 
whether within or beyond conscious awareness, can influence and 
facilitate the subsequent recognition of the same objects65–68 or how 
spatial regularities in a context can aid in the detection of embedded 
target stimuli69,70.
In summary, most behavioural tasks have focused on involuntary, 
incidental or mood-related emotional effects, limiting understanding 
of how emotion directly guides the perception of emotional stimuli. We 
highlight paradigms in which emotion-related attention and expecta-
tion provide explicit guidance, whereas contextual information offers 
implicit guidance of emotion perception. Emotion perception in these 
paradigms is measured by the detection, recognition or classification 
of an emotional stimulus measured using a variety of methods includ-
ing categorization, perceptual psychophysics and two-alternatives 
forced-choice tasks. Before reviewing the literature, we first highlight 
a key limitation in studies of emotion perception — the assumption 
that emotion-related biases are at play without directly quantifying 
them — which emphasizes the need for their explicit measurement.
Computational models
Computational models provide a more nuanced analysis of perception 
by quantifying the cognitive mechanisms that drive differences in speed 
and accuracy (Fig. 3). A vital point of concern is whether top-down 
influences predominantly pertain to perceptual processes or if they 
involve inferences, judgements and response biases50, a distinction 
that can be effectively parsed by employing computational models.
Signal-detection theory provides a natural framework for model-
ling the detection of emotional signals amid noisy backgrounds under 
the influence of attention and expectation54,71,72. In this process, an 
emotional stimulus is perceived when the signal surpasses a decision 
threshold relative to the noise. The subject’s performance can be quan-
tified via d′, which indexes sensitivity to occurrence of the emotional 
signal or ability to distinguish the emotional signal (true positive or 
hits) from noise (false alarms), and via c, which indexes the criterion and 
therefore a bias or tendency to report whether signal is present or not. 
Research on top-down influences on basic visual perception shows that 
attention towards a stimulus increases d′ to stimulus-related signals, 
whereas expectation of a stimulus shifts c, leading to a response bias 
towards endorsing the presence of the stimulus73–76. Given their sali-
ence, it can be hypothesized that attention or expectation of emotional 
stimuli enhances these effects.
Signal-detection theory provides a static snapshot of sensory 
signals, but the process of arriving at a perceptual decision takes 
time and can be modelled better via sequential sampling models that 
accumulate sensory evidence over time77,78. Drift-diffusion model-
ling assumes that noise-collapsed evidence is sampled stochastically 
and accumulated from a starting point between the two decision 
boundaries, until one of the decision boundaries is reached, result-
ing in a visual perceptual decision79,80. The rate at which evidence is

According to this framework, perception is the hypothesis or

for prior learning and context84,85. Prediction errors can result from 
inaccurate top-down predictions (for instance, predicting a snake that 
is absent) and/or noisy or imprecise sensory signals (for instance, a 
snake resting among fallen leaves and twigs). Hence, the precision of 
predictions, the sensory signal and prediction errors all play a part in 
shaping perception (Box 2).
The predictive processing framework has also been used to 
describe how conscious emotion arises from a dynamic interplay 
between predictions, interoceptive bodily signals and prediction 
errors87–90. The opposing impacts of top-down attention and expecta-
tion on prediction errors are debated60,91 but remain unexplored for 
emotional stimuli, which might differ from non-emotional stimuli 
owing to their salience and thereby the greater precision of sensory 
signals. Predictive coding accounts of salience in basic visual percep-
tion refer to the intrinsic precision of stimuli92–94. In line with these 
accounts, enhanced perception of emotional stimuli might result from 
the higher precision of prediction errors associated with emotional 
features, such as pronounced eye whites or mouth curvature95. This 
increased precision of emotion-related prediction errors is akin to an 
increased drift rate for an emotional stimulus in drift-diffusion mod-
elling because both reflect a greater reliability of the signal (sensory 
information or prediction error). By contrast, higher expectations 
regarding emotional stimuli increase the precision of related predic-
tions, thereby minimizing the impact of prediction errors and leading 
to a perceptual experience that aligns more closely with the expected 
outcome96.

Emotional top-down factors
Here we explore the commonalities and differences in top-down influ-
ences — both emotional and neutral — on the perception of emotional 
and neutral stimuli. Because expectation and attention can func-
tion independently and they have distinct psychological and neural 
mechanisms13,75, we discuss them separately. We review studies in which 
top-down emotion-related featural and spatial attention as well as 
expectations explicitly guide emotion perception while contextual 
knowledge implicitly shapes it. In contrast to the basic visual percep-
tion literature, the stimuli used in these studies tends to be threatening 
images such as angry faces or spiders or rewarding images such as 
food. Each subsection first examines the behavioural evidence, then 
explores computational models that quantify the underlying cogni-
tive mechanisms, and finally addresses the neural correlates of these 
processes.
Feature-based or object-based attention
Endogenous attention is guided by the observer’s internal goals, 
whereas exogenous attention is elicited by salient stimuli in the 
external environment. This attention can be directed towards spe-
cific features, such as colour or shape (feature-based attention), or 
towards entire objects, enhancing several features simultaneously 
(object-based attention). Traditionally, emotion-related attention 
tended to be equated with exogenous attention owing to evidence 
from behavioural tasks showing that both are involuntary and driven 
by stimulus features such as physical salience, looming nature, sudden 
or peripheral onset, and evolutionary salience30,98. However, consid-
erable research shows that preparatory attention, also known as the 
attentional set, can be endogenously directed towards features, objects 
or locations of upcoming target stimuli99,100. Surprisingly, this insight 
remained largely disconnected from studies on emotion perception 
until the past decade.
Top-down factors influence even relatively bottom-up or exog-
enous processing of emotional stimuli. For instance, exogenous cap-
ture of attention by emotional stimuli (such as threatening faces or 
spiders) in laboratory tasks occurs when they are the target of search, 
but not when they are in opposition to task goals101–104. Further, endog-
enous attention — as indexed by heightened amplitude of the P300 
event-related potential — becomes more pronounced when directed 
towards emotional targets than towards non-targets105–107.
The role of endogenous emotion-related attention in guiding 
perception has been examined in studies in which participants view 
pre-stimulus cues indicating that they should decide whether the 
upcoming stimulus (such as a face, matched for low-level stimulus 
features across the experiment) is fearful or neutral (Fig. 2). To emu-
late real-world perceptual challenges in noisy environments, the faces 
were perceptually degraded, prompting individuals to activate their 
attentional sets (‘fearful’ or ‘neutral’) in decision-making. Cues elicit-
ing a ‘fearful’ face set led to swifter and more accurate detection of 
subsequently presented fearful versus neutral faces, more so than the 
differential impact of neutral cues on corresponding neutral faces52,53,108. 
An examination of this data with signal-detection theory revealed that 
cues signalling threatening faces enhanced both the hit rate and percep-
tual sensitivity and that this increase was achieved without any alteration 
to the decision criterion54. Moreover, the application of drift-diffusion 
modelling showed that threat cues bias decision-making by shifting 
the initial starting point towards the decision boundary for endorsing 
threat, thereby allowing the participant to reach the threat decision 
faster55. This shift in starting point, even before the stimulus arrives, 
reflects a response bias and could potentially undermine the ability to 
distinguish between subsequent threatening and neutral stimuli. How-
ever, the threat-cue-related shift in starting point is balanced by a height-
ened drift rate or rate of evidence accumulation for both threatening 
and neutral stimuli, but more so for threatening stimuli55. Thus, the bias

one hidden by leaves, twigs and dirt (Fig. 1). The constraints of the 
sensory apparatus and the inherent computational imprecisions in 
the brain make sensory signals susceptible to perceptual noise224. 
Such uncertainty is considered intrinsic because it arises from 
sensory systems and differs from the extrinsic uncertainty that 
arises from variability in the environment225. Top-down predictions 
also exhibit varying degrees of uncertainty. Within the Bayesian 
brain hypothesis and predictive processing framework, the 
relative contribution or weight of bottom-up sensory evidence and 
top-down predictions is determined by their respective precision, 
which is inversely related to uncertainty85,86,226,227.
Vague prior knowledge (such as about the upcoming 
environment) can lead to uncertain top-down predictions, and 
therefore perception relies more heavily on the incoming sensory 
data84. In such cases, the quality of the sensory information becomes 
crucial in determining the accuracy of perception, with very precise 
sensory information driving perception86. Conversely, when the 
incoming sensory information is of low quality or ambiguous, 
indicating high extrinsic uncertainty, prior knowledge has a crucial 
role in perception. This reliance on prior knowledge can enhance 
the ability to perceive emotional stimuli accurately when prior 
knowledge aligns well with the sensory data, facilitating accurate 
perception. However, when there is a misalignment between prior 
knowledge and sensory data, the prior knowledge dominates 
perception owing to its lower uncertainty, and biases can arise.
These effects of uncertainty are relevant to the effects of 
attention and expectation highlighted in the main text. Attentional 
cues that indicate relevance but provide no probabilistic 
information regarding emotional stimuli (high uncertainty) enhance 
perceptual sensitivity and speed of detection of emotional stimuli 
more than neutral cues do55,54. However, cues indicating high 
probability (low uncertainty) regarding emotional stimuli shift 
the starting point of evidence accumulation towards the emotion 
decision boundary more than neutral cues shift the starting point of 
evidence accumulation towards the neutral boundary, suggesting 
an increased response bias55.

From a neural standpoint, the enhancement in perceptual 
decision-making due to threat cues arises from interactions between 
sensory, temporal and limbic regions during the anticipation of 
threatening stimuli and their subsequent processing. Before a person 
encounters the stimulus, anticipatory threat cues elicit a heightened 
late positive event-related potential and elevated blood oxygen-level 
dependent (BOLD) signal in the superior temporal sulcus, both of 
which are recognized indices of emotional face processing53. Notably, 
pre-stimulus increases in late positive potential and superior tem-
poral sulcus activity were predictive of subsequent enhancements 
in the speed and accuracy of perceptual decisions regarding threat-

thereby improving subsequent detection109–111. According to sequen-
tial sampling models such as drift-diffusion modelling, increases in 
pre-stimulus sensory activity might reflect a bias or shift in the start-
ing point for evidence accumulation towards a specific decision 
boundary13,112.
Prior information has also been shown to bias perceptual decision- 
making by enhancing the neural gain for the anticipated stimulus. 
These effects include context-related and attention-related gain for 
target stimuli and locations in primary and extrastriate visual cortices 
(for reviews see refs. 7,113,114). Additionally, emotional cues amplify 
amygdala activity only for subsequently presented threatening (and 
not neutral) faces53. The amygdala exhibits robust structural and func-
tional connections with the visual cortices115–117 and is hypothesized to 
implement gain control mechanisms for emotional stimuli118. Hence, 
the distinct activation pattern — responsive to emotional-cue-related 
top-down and stimulus-related bottom-up priorities — suggests that the 
amygdala regulates neural gain for emotional stimuli within the visual 
cortices. The notion of top-down emotion-related enhancement of neu-
ral gain aligns with the drift-diffusion modelling findings of faster drift 
rate or accumulation of sensory evidence for the emotional stimulus 
following emotional cues55. Along a similar vein, endogenous atten-
tion directed towards stimuli might increase the brain’s sensitivity to 
stimulus-related prediction errors13,93, a possibility that remains to be 
examined computationally for emotional stimuli.
A key question in this line of research is determining the origin of 
emotion-related top-down signals. On the basis of the individual’s goals, 
expectations or task demands, the prefrontal cortex sends top-down 
signals to sensory cortices to help prioritize certain stimuli or features 
for further processing. For instance, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex 
exhibits heightened activity when retaining task-relevant information 
that is essential for forthcoming decision-making119–121. Moreover, its 
activity is influenced by the motivational relevance of the information 
used for future decisions122,123. However, further research is needed to 
clarify how emotion-related top-down priorities are maintained in the 
dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and how this region modulates limbic 
and sensory regions in the service of emotion-related perception. 
Another part of the prefrontal cortex, the inferior frontal cortex, has 
also been shown to be involved in perceptual decision-making guided 
by top-down emotional priorities. Decision-making biased by top-down 
emotional sets versus neutral attentional sets activated different sub-
regions of the inferior frontal cortex compared to relatively unbiased 
decision-making with the same set of emotional and neutral stimuli124. 
Additionally, the inferior frontal cortex showed increased functional 
connectivity with the amygdala and sensory processing areas during 
unbiased but not during biased decision-making124. Thus, activation 
and functional connectivity patterns of different subregions might 
reflect differential strategies; biased emotion-related perceptual deci-
sions might rely on grosser features whereas unbiased decisions rely 
on finer-tuned features requiring more cross-talk between the inferior 
frontal cortex, amygdala and sensory areas. Overall, top-down atten-
tion to emotional stimuli enhances perception by increasing sensitivity, 
shifting the starting point, and boosting drift rate towards emotional 
decisions, while also engaging prefrontal, limbic and sensory regions 
more than neutral cues.
Spatial attention
Whereas feature-based attention involves the processing of specific 
stimulus attributes across the visual field, endogenous spatial atten-
tion refers to the voluntary, goal-directed allocation of attention to 
specific locations based on internal goals and expectations. The two 
rely on distinct but interacting neural mechanisms. Experimentally, 
spatial attention can be measured using endogenous cues that direct 
attention to the location of targets covertly (without the accompany-
ing eye movements) or before a saccade. For instance, studies in which 
participants fixated on a central point and a cue indicated the likely 
location of the upcoming emotionally or motivationally salient or 
neutral targets show a facilitative effect of spatial and emotional cues 
on perception56,125 (Fig 2). Cues that accurately predicted the location of 
emotional or motivationally salient targets resulted in faster detection 
of the targets compared to uninformative cues.
Further, similar to the pattern of activity observed following spa-
tially informative cues in basic visual perception57,126–129, cues that indi-
cate the location of emotional stimuli activate the frontoparietal spatial 
attention network, encompassing the intra-parietal sulcus, the frontal 
eye fields and the fusiform gyrus56. In addition, these cues activate brain 
regions associated with emotional processing, including limbic areas 
such as the amygdala56. The additive effects of spatial and emotional 
cues in the frontoparietal attention network indicate that this network 
seamlessly combines the spatial coordinates of a stimulus with its emo-
tional salience, creating a cohesive search template that primes sensory 
areas in anticipation of the upcoming stimulus130,131. This finding aligns 
with the idea that these neural networks represent salient aspects of 
the environment127,128,130,132. Furthermore, frontoparietal and sensory 
cortices exhibit increased connectivity with the amygdala after emo-
tional cues, which suggests that pre-stimulus threat-related cues evoke 
amygdala input into the spatial attention network and inferotemporal 
visual areas, thereby facilitating the detection of threatening stimuli56. 
This conclusion aligns with studies that show that the amygdala inte-
grates information about space and value, helping to direct atten-
tional resources towards salient stimuli in the environment133. Overall, 
top-down attention to the locations of emotional stimuli enhances 
perception more than to attention to neutral locations by engaging 
the frontoparietal attention network and amygdala.

providing information regarding the their probability (Fig. 2), on their 
perception. A majority of these studies have not independently manipu-
lated both the relevance (attention) and probability (expectation) of 
the emotional targets. Unlike the perceptual enhancement observed

be due to a response bias, such that individuals are more likely to make

when presented with probabilistic cues, observers tended to embrace 
a decision criterion that led to endorsing the presence of the expected 
stimulus, regardless of whether the cues signalled the impending

Drift-diffusion modelling analyses showed that high-probability 
emotion-related cues shift the starting point of evidence accumula-

do towards the neutral decision boundary, suggesting an increased 
response bias55. Notably, high-probability emotional cues typically 
did not facilitate perceptual sensitivity (the ability to discriminate 
between threatening and neutral stimuli) more than high-probability 
neutral cues54 did and when this facilitation did occur it was by boost-
ing the hit rates55. Although emotional cues increased the drift rate or 
evidence accumulation for subsequent stimuli, they did not exhibit a 
stronger effect for threatening compared to neutral targets. Similarly, 
in another study in which neutral targets underwent conditioning with 
either threatening or safe stimuli, cues that indicated a high probability 
of threatening and safe stimuli led to a more lenient decision criterion 
but had no impact on perceptual sensitivity. In contrast, acute threat 
induced by a threat of shock showed a different pattern of results for 
heightened expectations regarding neutral stimuli49 than what was 
seen for expectations regarding emotional stimuli55,137. Although the 
expectations enhanced perceptual sensitivity or discrimination of two 
neutral stimuli, they did not influence the decision criterion, leading the 
researchers to suggest that the influence of acute threat is to increase 
reliance on bottom-up sensory processing versus prior expectations 
in perceptual decision-making49.
Neural evidence regarding the influence of expectations on per-
ception comes mainly from basic perception research using neutral 
stimuli. Similar to increased anticipatory pre-stimulus BOLD that has 
been linked to top-down attention109–111, the influence of heightened 
expectations on perception involves increased anticipatory BOLD 
signal in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex138 and stimulus-specific sen-
sory cortices59,139–141, as well as greater connectivity between these two 
regions138. However, unlike attention, higher expectations are associ-
ated with decreased activity while processing the expected stimulus13,60, 
which is hypothesized to reflect a suppression of bottom-up sensory 
signals that are congruent with prior expectations142. Indeed, expec-
tation of emotional stimuli has been shown to increase pre-stimulus 
BOLD signal in the cingulate and parietooccipital, frontoparietal and 
dorsolateral prefrontal regions17,137. However, it did not influence the rel-
evant sensory cortices while the stimulus was being processed137. This 
lack of differential effect of emotion-related expectation on stimulus 
processing was also demonstrated in another study135.
In summary, unlike emotion-related attentional cues, emotion- 
related expectations do not differentially enhance perception com-
pared to neutral expectations, because both expectations shift the 
decision criterion and starting point without increasing perceptual 
sensitivity or drift rate.
Context
In the previous sections, we explored how explicit emotion-related 
attention and expectations influence perception. In the real world, 
threatening stimuli do not always occur in isolation nor do they con-
sistently follow explicit cues predicting their occurrence. Rather, 
threatening as well as neutral stimuli are embedded in a rich context 
of elements that implicitly indicate their relevance or probability. For 
example, the perceptual elements that constitute a rugged desert ter-
rain might indicate that snakes are relevant or likely, more so than the 
elements that constitute a trail in a city park, thereby influencing the 
detection of a snake in these environments. These contextual elements 
can therefore implicitly guide attention and expectations.
The powerful impact of context in shaping basic perception has 
been convincingly illustrated70. For instance, contextual knowledge 
enhances the recognition of stimuli that are relevant or likely within a 
given context but can lead to distortions when the stimuli are irrelevant 
or unlikely. Research exploring contextual influences on the perception 
of emotional stimuli has been somewhat constrained by a prevailing 
perspective that underscores the hardwired and universal nature 
of these stimuli and de-emphasizes the role of context143. However, 
research is increasingly demonstrating that emotional stimuli are also 
prone to contextual effects. For example, the perception of emotional 
expressions, is altered depending on the surrounding physical context 
including expressor-related aspects such as bodily expression and ele-
ments outside the expressor such as the scene elements144, perceiver 
characteristics such as linguistic or conceptual knowledge145–147, and 
the cultural context of the stimulus143,144,148,149.
Physical context can guide perception via learned associations 
between sensory stimuli and surrounding stimuli thanks to statistical 
regularities in the environment150. Humans extract and use knowledge 
of statistical regularities to guide the detection of visual targets151,152. 
Given that emotional stimuli form stronger associations with other 
stimuli than neutral stimuli do36 and humans’ enhanced ability to 
extract statistical regularities from emotional versus neutral scenes153, 
it stands to reason that contexts linked with emotional stimuli are 
learned better than those linked to neutral stimuli. Consequently, such 
emotional contexts can be used more effectively than neutral contexts 
to guide detection of associated targets. For instance, a dense forest 
environment associated with predators will facilitate their detection 
more than an urban park usually associated with harmless wildlife. The 
contextual cueing effect provides a way to examine how robust memory 
of regularities within visual contexts can guide faster detection of 
embedded targets compared to novel contexts154. In an emotional 
version of this task, detection of emotional targets is influenced not 
solely by stimulus characteristics but also by the acquired knowledge 
of the contexts in which threatening stimuli are encountered62,63,155. 
Further, when contexts linked to threatening targets are later associ-
ated with benign targets, it hinders learning the relationship with 
non-threatening targets, indicating the competitive advantage of 
threat-related contextual learning64.
By contrast, linguistic and conceptual knowledge can guide per-
ception by directing attention to aspects of sensory input that align 
with known words or concepts. For example, when looking for an 
‘angry face,’ one might focus on physical features such as exposed eye 
whites, furrowed brows or clenched teeth. During the search process, 
these features can be represented by a perceptual template, which

vivid emotion-related mental templates following emotional cues 
compared to neutral cues, thereby facilitating subsequent perception.
In summary, there are clear parallels between the patterns of 
top-down attention and expectations in emotional perception and 
basic visual perception. Emotion-related attentional cues enhance 
perception by increasing perceptual sensitivity, shifting the starting 
point, and boosting drift rate towards emotional responses. These 
cues also engage prefrontal, frontoparietal and sensory regions more 
strongly than neutral cues. Unlike attentional cues, emotion-related 
expectations do not differentially enhance perception compared to 
neutral cues, because both emotion-related and neutral expectations 
shift the decision criterion and starting point without increasing per-
ceptual sensitivity or drift rate. The lack of differential effects between 
emotion and neutral expectation cues might result from probabilistic 
information elevating neutral cues to the level of emotional cues. 
Additionally, research on economic decision-making shows that once 
the likelihood of a negative outcome exceeds zero, further increases 
in probability have little effect on emotions and decision-making15. 
This finding indicates a reduced sensitivity to changes in probability 
for emotional cues.
Finally, the context of emotional stimuli substantially influences 
how they are perceived. However, methodological challenges that 
complicate isolating the top-down effects of mood states on percep-
tion similarly hinder the investigation of contextual influences50. The 
context of an emotional stimulus encompasses attention, expectations 
and other factors that influence its perception; further research is 
needed to pinpoint specific contextual factors and their interactions. 
Moreover, the dependent variable in studies of contextual influence 
tends to be simple target detection, which makes it challenging to 
disentangle perceptual effects from variables like decision confidence 
and response bias.
Social top-down factors
Social top-down factors — encompassing interpersonal interactions, 
group dynamics and social norms — offer additional insights into per-
ception, attention and expectations in complex, real-world settings. 
Expectations about the probability of visual stimuli can be driven 
not only by true statistical covariation in the environment but also 
by learned sociocultural information, such as societal stereotypes and 
attitudes. Social information, including the social group memberships 
(such as gender and race) of a target person and the social and emo-
tional qualities of others in the immediate vicinity also provide con-
text for emotion perception. Moreover, individuals are embedded in 
larger collectives, which leads to broader affective and motivational 
processes — such as pressure to conform to peers or social norms and 
social fears such as the fear of being rejected — that can influence the 
perception of emotional stimuli. In this respect, some of the mecha-
nisms underlying social top-down influences might not be unique 
to the social domain (for instance, social stereotypes operating as 
basic top-down expectations that influence many other forms of visual 
perception60, while others might be more uniquely social in nature 
(for instance, the desire to socially conform164, which are goals or 
motives not generally at play in non-social perception).
Stereotypes can make categories such as race, gender and emotion 
psychologically intertwined, such that visual cues to these different 
dimensions mutually influence one another. For instance, men are 
stereotyped as prone to anger, whereas women are stereotyped as 
prone to joy and sadness165,166. Black individuals in the USA are also 
stereotypically associated with anger, owing to implicit associations 
with hostility and crime167,168. These stereotypical associations can 
become chronic expectations that exert top-down influences on the 
perception of the emotion expressed by a face or body24,169. Emotion-
ally ambiguous male faces are more likely to be perceived as angry, 
whereas the same emotional ambiguity on a female face is more likely 
to be perceived as happy165,170. Likewise, emotionally ambiguous Black 
faces are also more likely to be perceived as angry than are emotionally 
ambiguous white faces, particularly among perceivers who harbour 
stronger racial stereotypes167.
Moreover, even for clear and unambiguous emotional faces, gen-
der and racial stereotypes have a temporary biasing effect on the per-
ceptual process and the neural representational structure within the 
ventral visual stream; before perceptions are fully crystalized, faces 
are transiently represented in a manner more consistent with one’s 
stereotypical expectations than is ‘objectively’ the case171. This effect 
creates a kind of visual confirmation bias in which one’s pre-existing 
stereotypes are confirmed through visual perceptions. Stereotypes 
can also exert this kind of visual confirmation bias (on ventral visual 
representations of emotionally relevant objects, such as when racial 
stereotypes lead the brain to inaccurately represent innocuous tools 
as weapons (Oh, D., Vartiainen, H. & J.F., unpublished work) and lead 
to visually enhanced perception of weapons172. Neuroimaging stud-
ies show that these top-down effects of social conceptual associa-
tions on ventral-visual representations of facial emotion are related 
to functional connectivity between ventral-visual regions and the 
medial orbitofrontal cortex173, a region broadly involved in top-down 
expectations60. These interactions between medial orbitofrontal cortex 
and ventral-visual regions are broadly implicated in top-down impacts 
on object recognition, suggesting common mechanisms between 
social and non-social influences on perception24. There is also evidence 
for bottom-up routes for a limited set of these effects, such as a physical 
overlap in cues related to masculinity and anger as well as femininity 
and joy, and such featural overlaps in social cues themselves directly 
influence perception170. However, even when statistically controlling 
for physical overlap in stimuli, a top-down bias in ventral visual repre-
sentations of facial emotion is still observed171. Social-conceptual asso-
ciations also extend to perceivers’ learned understanding of different 
emotion categories. Greater conceptual similarity between emotion 
categories (such as anger and fear) leads facial expressions that convey 
those categories to be perceived more similarly, with increased neural

emotional states of others in the crowd to form a statistical average, 
which can then shape the perception of a specific target’s emotion181–183.

Metacognition and confidence
Observers can distinguish between perceptual decisions that are 
likely to be correct and those that are likely to be incorrect by relying 
on their subjective sense of confidence228. This metacognitive ability 
is critical in determining whether to promptly act on the decision, to 
persist in collecting more sensory information or to revise top-down 
expectations or attention on the basis of the available information229,230. 
Although some researchers propose that perceptual decisions and 
confidence share neural and computational substrates231,232, others 
have isolated neural and computational factors related to computation 
of perceptual confidence that are separate from the perceptual 
process229,233. Observers tend to be overconfident when judging 
emotional stimuli234,235. For example, they demonstrate overestimation 
of accuracy of emotion recognition, compared to judgements of 
visual size234. Discrimination of emotional facial expressions recruits 
regions in the face-processing network, whereas confidence regarding 
the discrimination recruits the retrosplenial and posterior cingulate 
cortices234, indicating the involvement of different neural mechanisms 
for perceptual and confidence judgements.
Interoception
When faced with an emotional stimulus, the human body undergoes 
various physiological changes, and awareness of these changes 
can shape perception236. Interoception involves the perception 
of physiological signals originating from within the body237. 
Exteroception — perception of external stimuli — bears a strong 
bidirectional and complex relationship with interoception. For 
example, the interpretation of internal physiological arousal 
as fear can heighten hypervigilance, enabling the detection of 
threatening or ambiguous external stimuli238. Alternatively, external 
emotional stimuli can induce physiological responses that could 
be perceived as anxiety, regardless of their true nature238. For 
instance, during cardiac systole — when the heart sends a signal to 
the brain — emotional stimuli are detected more quickly, judged as 
more intense and trigger a stronger amygdala response than during 
diastole239. Similarly, breathing phase has been shown to enhance 
fear discrimination: fearful faces were detected more quickly during 
nasal inspiration than during expiration240.
Furthermore, the amplitude of anticipatory heart-rate-evoked 
potential — a neural marker of cardiac interoception — is correlated 
with heightened sensitivity in detecting an ensuing neutral 
stimulus241. Interestingly, whereas the expectation of a neutral 
stimulus elevated the amplitude of the heart-rate-evoked potential, 
expectation of an emotional stimulus decreased its amplitude242,243, 
an effect that was magnified when participants were explicitly 
aware of predictive cues244. This finding indicates that interoception 
following emotional expectations differs from neutral expectations, 
and that this difference is influenced by top-down awareness. The 
reduced heart-rate-evoked potential amplitude was associated with 
better detection of the emotional stimulus as well as lower neural 
activity indexing visual processing. As in the exteroceptive domain142, 
this reduction in heart-rate-evoked potential is hypothesized 
to reflect a sharpening of sensory processing by suppressing 
bottom-up sensory prediction errors that are incongruent with prior 
expectations.

Overall, the mechanisms behind social impacts on perception of 
emotional stimuli in some cases reflect more general mechanisms of 
an adaptive perceptual system attuned to prior knowledge and context 
(as with stereotypes operating as top-down expectations, which readily 
occur in non-social perception too) or in other cases reflect mecha-
nisms uniquely social in nature (as with the affective and motivational 
processes that uniquely arise in social contexts or among collectives 
of people). Delineating these different mechanisms is important for 
interventions that aim to reduce these influences where they are harm-
ful and maladaptive (for instance, social stereotyping) or support them 
where they are helpful and adaptive (for instance, drawing on social 
context or one’s peers to perceive emotion).
Summary and future directions
Fast and accurate detection of emotional stimuli is crucial for adaptive 
interactions with the environment and enables timely and appropri-
ate responses to social and situational cues. Drawing on theoretical 
perspectives that support top-down influences, computational frame-
works that formalize these influences, and behavioural paradigms 
grounded in cognitive neuroscience12–14, we have reviewed how 
emotion-related top-down factors guide the perception of emotional 
stimuli. There are striking parallels with basic studies that focus on 
the perception of relatively neutral stimuli, highlighting similar com-
putational and neural mechanisms. Finally, the social aspects of the 
environment exert a strong top-down influence on the perception of 
emotional stimuli. Emphasizing top-down factors in emotion percep-
tion is essential for developing comprehensive models that integrate 
top-down with bottom-up influences. Such models are more ecologi-
cally valid because they align with the use of prior knowledge and con-
text in everyday perception and are translatable to clinical settings 
because they incorporate prior learning and schemas in perception.
Important issues for future research involve determining how 
early the top-down effects of emotional information are observed in 
the human visual processing hierarchy. One perspective suggests that 
top-down influences on feedforward pathways emerge only after the 
essential features of sensory events have been encoded. This perspec-
tive is supported by evidence that higher associative cortices have very 
few monosynaptic projections to primary sensory areas and send fewer 
projections upstream than to downstream components of unimodal 
areas128. Another perspective proposes that there is an adaptive advan-
tage in altering neural responses to lines, edges, shapes and colours. 
This is supported by evidence from other species such as frogs, in which 
attention can dramatically influence response of visual neurons as early 
as first synapse from receptors186. More research is needed to reconcile 
these perspectives with respect to emotional stimuli.
Other key future research areas involve the relationship of emotion 
perception with other aspects of cognition. Because emotional stimuli 
are linked to stronger memories and visual associative memories have 
a crucial role in perception150, it is important to examine whether and 
how visual associative memory for emotional stimuli influences per-
ception differently than neutral stimuli. Similarly, because predictions 
and expectations are maintained in working memory187, examining the 
role of working memory in emotion-related facilitation of perception is 
crucial. Although the mechanisms of top-down attention and expecta-
tion for emotional stimuli might differ13,75, it remains to be seen whether 
the two are orthogonal and whether their effects can be isolated in the 
context of emotional stimuli. Further research is also needed on the role 
of interoception and decision confidence, as both contribute to how 
individuals evaluate and interpret emotional stimuli based on bodily 
signals and perceived certainty in their judgments (Box 3).
Finally, research that investigates top-down influences on emotion 
perception offers invaluable insights into the cognitive and affective 
mechanisms underlying mental disorders such as anxiety and depres-
sion. Predominant frameworks for understanding and treating depres-
sion and anxiety disorders revolve around top-down maladaptive 
schemas or belief systems26,27. Diagnostic criteria for these disorders 
emphasize anxiety as an anticipatory response to potential or future 
threats188. Empirical research shows that anxiety is characterized by 
hypervigilance for threats, whether a threatening stimulus is present 
or not29,189–191. Anxiety involves an overestimation of the likelihood and 
the potential costs of negative events192 and depression is associated 
with higher expectations regarding future negative events193,194. These 
factors collectively emphasize the need to examine the influence of
